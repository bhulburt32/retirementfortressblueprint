Running a Fortress Blueprint

How to take a household from a list of statements to a client-ready Retirement Fortress design in about fifteen minutes.

Internal use Twenty-one sections, top to bottom No login, nothing installed
StartOrientation

What the tool does

You give it a household's income picture and a list of their existing accounts. It sizes the four Fortress levels from those numbers, then maps every account to a destination — which registration it consolidates into, which carrier it ends up with, and what stays put. Out the other end come six documents: five for the client, one for the file.

It does not replace your judgment. The allocation it proposes is a starting point you are expected to override, and section 14 exists for exactly that.

Nothing is saved

The tool keeps no data. Close the tab or hit refresh and the case is gone — there is no autosave and no account. Before you walk away, go to section 22, press Copy case data, and paste the block into your CRM note. That block is the only way back.

Everything opens with a +

The page loads with all twenty-one sections closed — a column of navy headers. Click a header, or its +, to open a section; inside, every subsection bar and every standalone table has its own + as well, and the year-by-year tables sit behind a + of their own. Expand all sections and Collapse all sections at the top do it in one go. Any Add a … button opens whatever it adds to, so a new row is never hidden. Every action button on the page is green; the only exceptions are the × that removes a row and the +/− toggles.

The page opens with the default case loaded — the Smith household, a saved case built in so you can see how every section behaves, with a transfer plan, three scenarios and a Nitrogen analysis already in place. Press Start new case in the top right — twice, it asks you to confirm — to empty every field. Your name and the date carry over; the client's details do not. To open with a different case, paste its block from section 22 into Load pasted case; to change the default itself, the saved block is swapped into the page.

The header carries The Road to Retirement Fortress, a short video of the Fortress story for the client meeting or as a follow-up link; it opens in a new tab on claude.ai, where a signed-in Alto account is needed.

Every section of the application opens with User Guide Notes under its header — the same instructions as this guide's section, collapsed until you open them with the +. They are copied from this guide, so the two never disagree.

LevelsReference

The four levels, and how the tool sizes them

The framework in one table — the same description appears in the executive summary report. Send clients to the whitepaper for the full version.

Level What it holds How the tool sizes it
Roof Wishes Growth and legacy assets The residual — whatever the other three do not need
Walls Wants Balanced market investments The higher of 24 months of Needs, or (Wants less non-guaranteed income) ÷ the Walls yield
Foundation Needs Cash buffer, plus guaranteed income Buffer = months × Needs ÷ 12. Annuity = the Needs gap over the first 15 years of retirement — the highest month as a year, or the average, per section 03 (projected before the plan: Needs with inflation against Social Security with its COLA, pensions and annuity income) ÷ the Foundation yield
Moat Protection Long-term care contracts Benefit ÷ an age ratio: 3.2 at 55, 3.17 at 60, 3.1 at 65, interpolated for every age between and beyond

The Moat here is narrower than the framework's

In the whitepaper the Moat defends against long-term care and tax drag. This tool sizes only the long-term care piece. Withdrawal sequencing, Roth conversions and asset location are still your job, and the executive summary report says the tax side is handled in how and when income is drawn.

01Input

Prepared by

The first thing on the page: your name and the date. Both print on the client PDF and the worksheet, and the date sets the plan's first year for the projections. The Advisor name carries from case to case; the Date prepared resets to today whenever you start a new case. Advisor cell and Advisor email print with your name on every report — the executive summary cover, the initial observations and findings (header and closing contact line, with Alto's number and website), the Social Security, risk profile and pension reports and the worksheet. The cell is shown as (nnn) nnn-nnnn; leave either blank and it is left off.

02Input

Client information

Who the plan is for. Names print on every document and drive the filenames.

Client and spouse
First and last name, date of birth, age and sex for each. Enter the Date of birth and the age is calculated — whole years at the plan date in section 01 — and the age cell follows it; leave the date blank and the age is typed as before. The date also fixes the exact 59½ date for the early-withdrawal penalty in section 16 and feeds the Social Security optimizer and the pension calculator in section 04 when their own date is blank. Age and sex set the long-term care benefit defaults in sections 08 and 09. Leave every spouse field blank for a single client and nothing in the plan is titled Joint or Spouse.
State of residency
Sets the state income tax model in section 16 and is recorded for the file. Product approval is by state, so check it against the carrier before anything in section 19 goes to paper.
Dependents
Add adult dependent and Add minor dependent take a first name and an age each. They do not enter the Fortress sizing, but their ages drive the life insurance need in section 08 — years of income replacement and education funding — and they travel with the case so the file is complete.
03Input

Plan assumptions

The rates and choices the whole plan is built on, kept in one place so they are easy to review before a client sees anything. They are planning assumptions, not quotes, and the executive summary report's disclosures carry whatever you set here.

Liquidity buffer
Months of Needs held in cash. Defaults to 6. Sized on full Needs, not the gap.
Foundation sizing method and window (N)
How the Needs gap that sizes the Foundation annuity is measured, and over how many years of retirement: Average Needs gap over N years (the default) or Peak monthly Needs gap in the first N years, with N 5 by default. The tool runs the status-quo projection — nothing moved, no annuity bought — and for each of the first N full calendar years with everyone retired takes that year's Needs (growing with inflation, dated budget lines honoured) less that year's Social Security with its COLA, pensions and annuity income already in place. On the peak method the largest of those gaps — the highest monthly shortfall, taken as a year in the dollars of that year — divided by the Foundation yield is the annuity target, so a level payment covers the gap in every year of the window and runs ahead of it in the others. On the average method the mean of the gaps is used instead: a smaller target that runs a little behind the gap in the costliest years and ahead in the rest. Section 04 shows today's gap and the other measure beside the sizing figure, and the section 13 note gives the peak year. Shorten the window to size on the early years, lengthen it to cover more of the inflation drift.
Yields
Foundation yield and Walls yield, both 6.0% by default. The Foundation yield turns the sizing Needs gap into a Foundation target; the Walls yield turns Wants into a Walls target. Change them if the products you actually place justify it.
Guaranteed lifetime income
The default for every Foundation annuity in the projections; section 14 can set each contract on its own. A contract's value falls by the income it pays each year either way (no crediting is assumed). With Yes — the default, a lifetime-income contract — the payment continues for life after the value reaches zero. With No the contract pays a monthly share of its value and the payment stops once the value is gone, so Foundation income drops in the years that follow and the Roof carries more of Needs. Sections 15 and 17 and the executive summary report all follow the setting; the sizing in section 13 does not change.
Growth rates
Walls growth rate (1.5% by default) is what the Walls balance is assumed to grow at after its income is drawn, and Roof growth rate (6.0%) is the Roof's assumed growth. Neither changes the sizing in section 13; both drive the projection chart there — Walls income rising with the Walls, and the Roof balance column.
Inflation
What Needs and Wants grow at in the projection. Defaults to 3.26%. The tax projection in section 16 indexes the brackets and deductions at the same rate.
End of plan age
The age of the younger client when the plan ends; 100 by default. It sets how far the income projection in section 13 and the tax projection in section 16 run.
Medicare
Medicare Part B and Medicare Part D, monthly per person. They default to the 2026 figures — the $202.90 standard Part B premium and the $38.99 Part D base beneficiary premium — and feed the Medicare lines in the section 05 worksheet. Standard premiums only: a household over the IRMAA thresholds pays more, so raise them for that client.
New custodian
Where Step 1 of the transfer sequence consolidates everything: Schwab or Fidelity.
Life insurance cash value
No leaves existing policies alone. Yes puts their cash value toward the Moat by 1035 exchange — and because a 1035 has to keep the same owner, each policy can only fund that person's own contract.
04Input

Income

The income side of the sizing — this section and the Needs and Wants in section 05 together drive everything downstream, so both are worth getting right before you touch the accounts.

It opens with an Employment income table, one column per person: annual salary, target retirement date, annual payroll deductions, the employee and employer retirement contributions as percentages of salary, which account from section 06 those contributions land in, and the annual salary increase. Beneath it the tool works out each person's net before-tax annual salary (salary less payroll deductions and the employee contribution), the dollars going into retirement accounts each year, and the time to retirement with the salary projected to that date. These describe the working years and are recorded for the plan; the Fortress sizing runs off the retirement figures that follow.

Under it, Additional employment income takes any number of further paychecks for either person — a second job, part-time or consulting work in retirement, a board seat. Each line has a name, whose it is, the gross annual amount in today's dollars, a start date (blank: already paying), an end date (blank: that person's target retirement date when they have one still ahead, otherwise through the plan) and an annual increase. The note beneath each line reads it back, and the hint totals what is paying now. These incomes count as wages on their own dates in the income gap, the tax projection, the status quo and the reports (they appear as rows on the Employment income table of the executive summary and in the Household income tile); no payroll deductions or retirement contributions are assumed on them, and they do not move the retirement dates. They save with the case.

Next comes Social Security and pension income. For Social Security enter each person's Primary Insurance Amount from their SSA statement, the age they will start benefits (62 to 70) and one COLA assumption (2.5% by default); the tool applies the SSA's own early-claiming reduction or delayed credits against a full retirement age worked out from the age in section 02, and shows the monthly benefit in today's dollars and what COLA makes of it by the start age. When the lower earner's PIA is under half of the higher earner's, the tool adds the spousal top-up — half the higher PIA less their own, reduced on the SSA's spousal schedule if claimed early, with no delayed credits — and notes that it is payable only once the higher earner has filed.

Under the Social Security table, the Claiming optimizer tests every claiming age from 62 to 70 (plus full retirement age) for each person — 72 combinations for a couple — paying each one out month by month to an assumed lifespan with the SSA's early-claiming reductions and delayed credits, the spousal top-up and deemed filing, and the survivor benefit (the larger of the two, with the 82.5% floor), COLA applied each January. It takes the PIAs, ages, names and COLA already on the page; you add only each person's life expectancy (blank: 85 for a man, 88 for a woman), an optional discount rate (blank ranks nominal totals; equal to the COLA ranks in today's dollars) and, the section 02 dates of birth (read-only here while section 02 has them; otherwise optional dates here) for month-level precision — otherwise ages are whole years from section 02. With Rank with the trust-fund stress test ticked the strategies are ranked on the 22%-from-2032 reduction used in section 18, with the no-cut total alongside. Tiles name the top strategy, its lifetime benefit, the surviving spouse's income under it, and where the plan's own claiming ages rank among everything tested; the table lists the five best, then four strategies that always show with their true rank (shaded): File early, both at 62; File late, both at 70; the client early at 62 and the spouse late at 70; the client late at 70 and the spouse early at 62 — a person already past an age files at the earliest age still open to them — each with the person's monthly amounts, lifetime totals and the age at which it catches up to filing earliest; a chart runs the cumulative benefits (the plan's own ages as a dashed line when they are not among the strategies shown), and Break-even ages opens the full strategy-versus-strategy table. Use the top strategy in the plan sets the benefit starting ages above to strategy #1 (whole years), so the income projections, the Foundation sizing and every report follow. The optimizer's settings save with the case. Not modelled: the earnings test, tax on benefits, WEP/GPO, restricted applications, disability or dependent benefits.

Pensions are a list: Add a pension for each one either person will receive — a name, whose it is, the monthly amount in today's dollars, start date, COLA and the survivor percentage (0 to 100) — with a note under each line and totals by person. Other guaranteed income is a third list for annuities already paying out or scheduled to — Add an annuity income gives a name, whose it is, the monthly amount, start date, an end date or Life, COLA, survivor percentage and the account in section 06 that holds the contract; a contract whose end date has passed is shown but left out of the total. All three roll straight into the household figures the Foundation is sized from — there is nothing to copy or apply. A fourth list, Non-guaranteed income, records rents, royalties, part-time work or business income — name, whose (including joint), monthly amount, start, end or Ongoing, annual increase and a note. It never closes the Foundation gap, which only guaranteed income may do, but it does pay for Wants: the Walls are sized on Wants less this income, so a rental covering part of the discretionary budget shrinks the Walls target accordingly (the 24-month Needs floor still applies).

The Pension Benefits Calculator beneath the pension list estimates a public pension from the plan formula — the systems in Alto's Pension Reference Guide: PSERS and SERS, VRS, SRPS, TSERS, SCRS, TPAF, MTRS, MPSERS, TRS (Illinois and Texas), the three Ohio systems, PERSI, FERS and CSRS, and Wespath. Pick whose pension it is and the State / pension system, enter the date of birth and the expected retirement date — both come from sections 02 and 04 while blank, so the age at retirement is the plan's — and the employment start (the age at retirement and the years of service are calculated; type over the years if the record says otherwise), the beneficiary's age, then the Membership class / tier (the hire date suggests one), the average final compensation over the years the formula uses, any split-period service the class asks for, and the Benefit option. Calculate estimated benefit shows the monthly and annual benefit, the single-life maximum, the income replacement, the eligibility rule met, the formula, the system's COLA note and a warning where the system does not pay into Social Security (WEP and GPO). Add to pension income then puts a line in the pension list — the monthly benefit from the retirement month, the option's survivor percentage, COLA 0% until you set one — and the projections follow. The survivor factors are general estimates and every figure carries the reference tool's disclaimer: verify with the plan administrator before recommending an option.

The section closes with Your income today and in retirement, the same summary the executive summary report carries: four tiles — household income today, guaranteed income in retirement, the share of Needs it covers and the Annual Needs income gap the Foundation annuity is sized to close — a table setting Social Security, pensions and annuity income against Annual Needs, and a Sources of income chart stacking salaries, Social Security, pensions, annuity and non-guaranteed income year by year as the plan stands today, before the Fortress is built. The note beneath gives the first full retirement year's figures; the shortfall against expenses is the Income Gap chart in section 05.

Carriers
There is no carrier field to fill. The Foundation annuity is assumed to go to the carrier of the largest annuity in section 06, and the Moat contract to the carrier of the largest life or LTC policy there. No such account, or no custodian typed on it, and the documents read "TBD" with a flag until you pick a carrier on the line in section 14 — fine for a first pass, not for anything you hand over.
05Input

Expenses

The section opens with the two figures everything is sized from, and the detailed worksheet folded away beneath them.

Annual Needs
Essential expenses — what the household costs before anything optional. This one number drives the buffer, the Foundation and the Walls floor.
Annual Wants
Travel, gifts, entertainment. What they would rather not cut first. The Walls are sized on Wants less any non-guaranteed income from section 04.

Type both straight in from a client's own figures, or press Enter detailed expenses to open Alto's monthly budget worksheet, line for line — six categories from Household to Entertainment & other, with three Other lines you can name. Enter monthly amounts. Each line carries a Need / Want switch, preset the way most households would answer (mortgage and groceries are Needs; dining out and travel are Wants); change any of them. Start and end dates and notes are for the file — a mortgage that ends in 2031, a tuition line that starts next autumn — and do not change the arithmetic.

Under Healthcare & insurance the worksheet carries six lines it fills for you. Life Insurance and Long Term Care Insurance take the monthly premiums of the policies listed in sections 08 and 09 (grey) until you type an amount — do that when the premiums are already in the budget. The other four: Medicare Part B and Part D for each person, at the premiums set in section 03, with a start date of the month each person turns 65 worked out from the ages in section 02 (blank once they are 65 already). They are shown in grey while they are defaults; type over an amount or a start date and that line is yours and stops following. A single client gets two lines, not four. On their own the Medicare defaults do not count as a budget — the apply button waits for a line you entered yourself — but they are in the totals from the first line you add.

Or let the client fill in Alto's Monthly Budget Worksheet — the Budget worksheet button in the app's header saves the blank fillable PDF to send them — and Upload the completed Monthly Budget Worksheet — drop the PDF on the box under the toggle or choose it. The worksheet is a fillable form, and the page reads the form fields themselves, so a copy completed in Acrobat, Preview or a browser reads the same. Page 1 lands line for line in the detailed expenses: every worksheet line replaces what the budget held (a blank line clears it), the Need / Want switch keeps its preset, dates in the usual forms (06/2031, Oct 2027, 2027-05) become start and end dates and anything else is kept in the note, and a note on an Other line becomes that line's name. The Life Insurance and Long Term Care Insurance lines keep following the policies unless the worksheet gives an amount. Page 2 goes to Employment income in section 04, a column each: the payroll deductions (everything withheld except the retirement contributions) become the annual Payroll deductions, and the employee contributions and the employer match and profit sharing become the two percentages of the salary already entered there — enter the salary first, or the message tells you the dollar amounts it could not convert. Employer HSA contributions, pension accruals and IRA contributions outside payroll are reported in the message and not carried. The worksheet opens and the totals update; press Apply (or keep the sync ticked) for Annual Needs and Wants to follow it.

Under the table the budget rolls up into annual Needs and Wants next to what the two fields above currently hold. Apply to Annual Needs & Wants copies them up once; tick Keep Annual Needs & Wants in sync and every budget edit updates the fields as you type. Leave both alone and the fields keep whatever you entered. Hide detailed expenses folds the worksheet away again; a line beside the button keeps the monthly total and the two annual figures in view, and a case with budget lines reopens with the worksheet showing.

The worksheet is optional

It is there for the household that has not done the budgeting work. Everything in it saves and reloads with the case.

Start and end dates are honoured in the projections. While the budget is the source of Annual Needs and Wants — kept in sync, or the two figures still equal to its totals — every year-by-year projection (the income gap, the status quo, the Fortress projection, the tax table) counts each line only between its start and end months, pro-rated to the month in the first and last year: a mortgage line ending June 2041 is half a year's payments in 2041 and nothing after, Medicare begins the month each person turns 65, and a line with no dates runs for the whole plan. The annual totals shown in the section, and the Foundation sizing in section 13, still use every line in full — so the Foundation is sized to today's Needs as before. Type over Annual Needs or Wants and the projections fall back to those flat figures. Debt maturities in section 07 are independent: end the budget line yourself when the loan clears.

The first plan year is prorated. The plan starts in the month of the Date prepared in section 01, so the first row of every projection carries only the rest of that calendar year — three months for a plan prepared in October — for income and expenses alike: salaries, Social Security, pensions, annuity and other income, the Walls yield and the Foundation annuity on the income side, Needs and Wants (and each dated budget line) on the other. Income tax for that year is still figured on the whole calendar year, since brackets are annual: the plan carries its share of the year's tax in proportion to its income (the rest was withheld before the plan), and any Roth conversion in that year is taxed at the margin on top of the full year's income — so the converter sees the real headroom, not a quarter-year's. The assumptions notes under the projections say which month the plan runs from.

Beneath the budget sits the Income Gap chart: every source of income as it stands today — salaries to each retirement date, Social Security, pensions, annuity and other income on their own dates — as a blue bar for each year, total expenses as a line, and the shortfall where expenses outrun income drawn in red on top of the bar, labelled every fifth year. The tiles above it give the year the gap first appears, the gap at full retirement, the largest gap and the total over the plan: the figure the Fortress is built to fund. It runs before any Fortress is built, so advisor adjustments in section 14 do not move it; the same chart is the Income Gap page of the executive summary report.

06Input

Assets

Opening the section opens the account list with it. Three ways in. Pick whichever suits the case:

  1. Upload a spreadsheet. Press Download template for the Alto account template, fill it in, then drop it on the panel or use Choose file. You get a preview before anything lands — then Replace table or Add to table. CSV works too.

  2. Paste rows. Open Or paste rows instead and paste straight from a spreadsheet. Tab- or comma-separated, same column order, header row skipped automatically.

  3. Type them. Add account for one row at a time. Fastest for a household with four or five accounts.

Two columns decide more than they look like they do:

  • Tax type drives the whole waterfall. Qualified, Roth and Non-Qualified money go to different levels in a different order — get this wrong and the design is wrong.
  • Current position tells the tool what it is looking at. Mark bank savings and money market as Cash — that is what the liquidity buffer keys on, and a household with no Cash row gets no buffer. The five annuity positions — MYGA Annuity, Variable Annuity, Income Annuity (FIA), Growth Annuity (FIA) and RILA Annuity (a plain Annuity from an older case loads as a MYGA) — and the two policy positions, Cash Value Life Insurance and LTC / Life (handled alike: never drawn on, a 1035 into the Moat or left in place), get their own handling for assignments and 1035s, every annuity position lists its contract in the Annuities panel below, and each type follows its own rules in the projections (see the panel). An uploaded or pasted position such as MYGA, variable annuity, FIA with income rider, fixed indexed annuity, RILA or SPIA resolves to the matching type. An account set to Income Annuity (FIA) is always Foundation: it is already paying, so it stays with its carrier, counts in the Foundation and its income counts in the Foundation sizing, whatever its Transfer? column or its section 14 lines say.

The Transfer? column at the end holds an account back. Set it to Stay as is and that account still takes its place in the Fortress levels — the waterfall (or your section 14 lines) puts it in a level and the projections count it there — but it stays at its institution, generates no paperwork, and drops out of the billable base; the fee note names the amount so nothing hides. The one thing a held account never does is buy a Moat contract, which needs a 1035. (Stay put, no transfer on a section 14 line is different: that holds the money out of every level.)

The Growth column is each account's assumed annual growth. Leave it blank (default) and the account takes the plan rate from section 03 for the level its money lands in — the Walls rate for the Walls and for anything that stays as is, the Roof rate for the Roof. Type a rate and it is blended into that level's growth in proportion to the account's share, so the projections in sections 16, 15 and 17 follow it; the assumptions line under each one shows the blended rates.

Annuities
A panel under the accounts with one row per account whose current position is an annuity: the owner and account from the list, then Company (follows the custodian until typed), Product, Type (the account's current position — change it here or in the list, the other follows), Rate and Interest (deferred and accumulating, or taken as income) for a MYGA, Maturity date, Monthly income and Payout (single life or joint) for an income annuity, Fees as a percent a year, Cap rate for the FIAs and a RILA, Participation rate for the FIAs and Floor (the most a bad year can lose) for a RILA; cells that do not apply to the type are greyed out. The details save with the case and print in the worksheet under Annuity contracts.

The projections follow these cells. An income annuity in force is always Foundation: it stays with its carrier and counts in the Foundation (it sits on its own Foundation line in section 14 and under the Foundation in section 15); it pays its monthly income from now — the amount typed here, else a section 04 line linked to the account, else the Foundation yield on its value — for the owner's life when single (to the owner's end-of-plan age) or to the end of plan when joint, its value falls by what it pays and the income continues after the value is gone; the Foundation is sized net of that income; it takes no loss in the bad-years stress test. A MYGA grows at its rate when one is typed (the plan rate otherwise); with the interest taken as income it pays the rate on its value each year as annuity income and does not grow; at its maturity date the interest stops and the money is market money in its level from then on, at the level's rate and exposed to the stress test; before maturity it keeps earning its rate in the bad years. A variable annuity is market money: it grows at the plan rate less its fees and takes the full stress loss. A growth FIA grows at the plan rate up to its cap, and sits at zero in a bad year. A RILA grows at the plan rate up to its cap, and in a bad year loses no more than its floor. The Walls and Roof blend these account by account, as they already do for a rate typed in the Growth column, so the level rates and the stress returns in sections 17 and 18 reflect what each level holds.

Other assets
A second panel under the accounts for real assets outside the Fortress — primary residence, vacation home, rental property, land, a business interest, vehicles or other real assets — each with a value and an assumed appreciation rate. They do not enter the levels or the projections (nothing in them is invested), but they complete the balance sheet: the Net worth readout is the accounts plus these, less the debts in section 07, and both print in the worksheet. Rent from a rental property belongs in Non-guaranteed income in section 04, and the mortgage on a home in section 07.
Projected assets before the Fortress
The status quo, for comparison with section 17: every account left where it is and grown at its own rate (or, by default, cash flat, annuities at the Walls rate and market accounts at the Roof rate), current income — salary to each retirement date, Social Security, pensions, annuity and non-guaranteed income — against the Needs and Wants budget growing with inflation, and any gap drawn in the section 16 withdrawal order with the tax on the draw. Five tiles, a stacked bar by tax type for every year with the retired years shaded and the first RMD year marked, a note that sets the end-of-plan figure against the Fortress projection, and a year-by-year table behind a +. It runs on the same engine as section 17 and prints in the worksheet.
If a long term care event occurs
Under the status-quo chart, the same projection run again with a care event added, as a set of three charts: the male needs care, the female needs care, both need care (one chart for a single client). Costs come from the section 12 estimate — the state medians or the costs typed there, the average durations for a man and a woman, the care start age and the cost inflation — the full projected cost when one person needs care and the full combined cost when both do, net of any benefits already in force. With no Fortress in place the whole cost comes out of the accounts in the withdrawal order, with the tax on the draw. Each chart carries tiles for the cost of care, what is drawn from the accounts and the end-of-plan figure (or the year the accounts run out), and the projection without a care event as a dashed line for comparison. Each person who needs care is assumed to pass away at the end of the care period: from then the survivor receives the larger of the two Social Security benefits, single-life annuity income and any salary of the deceased stop, and pensions and section 04 annuity lines pay their survivor percentage (an annuity line's 100% means joint); expenses, the tax filing status and RMDs are unchanged. When no one is left the plan ends: no further income, expenses, tax or withdrawals, and the balances stand as the estate, which the tile labels Estate from the year. The charts need a current age in section 02 for each person.
07Input

Debts

One line per obligation — mortgage, HELOC, credit card, signature loan, auto loan, student loan or other — with the lender or a description, the balance, the annual interest rate, the monthly payment and the maturity (leave it blank for a card or any revolving line). Add a debt starts a new line; the × removes one.

Under each line the tool works out how much of the payment is interest at today's balance, how long that payment takes to clear the debt and how much more interest it costs on the way, and whether that lands before, after or in line with the maturity. A line at 10% or more is marked as high-rate debt. The footer totals the balances, the weighted rate and the payments, and the readout splits mortgage and HELOC balances from everything else.

Where the numbers go

The balances carry straight into section 08: while this section has a line, the Mortgage balance and Other debts fields in the life insurance analysis read from here and cannot be typed over. The payments are not added to the expense budget in section 05 — the budget is your own worksheet, so enter them there if it should carry them. Debts travel with the case and print in the worksheet under Debts.

08Input

Life Insurance

Alto's Coverage Needs Analyzer, built in with a column for each person. The section opens with the two Existing life insurance fields — the death benefit already in force, individual and group combined — and a readout of the additional coverage recommended; Complete detailed life insurance needs analysis opens the rest, and a case that used it reopens with it showing.

Existing life insurance policies, at the top of the section, lists each policy in force: owner, company, insured, death benefit, beneficiary, type (Term, Whole Life, IUL), cash value (permanent policies), years remaining (term) and the monthly premium, with Add a life insurance policy for each. While any are listed, the death benefits by insured fill the Existing life insurance fields (grey, read from the list) and the needs analysis counts them as coverage in force; the premiums of all policies fill the Life Insurance line of the section 05 budget until an amount is typed there — type one when the premiums are already in the budget. Everything saves with the case.

Household obligations
Mortgage balance, Other debts and College savings. They are owed whichever person dies first, so they count in full against each person's need.

Strategic Tax Planning

Everything in this section runs on the adjusted allocation in section 15 — the plan as it stands after your transfers in section 14, not the model's own in section 13. Change a line there and the withdrawal draws, the Roth conversion schedule and the year-by-year table all re-run.

How retirement income is drawn for tax: the withdrawal order, a Roth conversion analysis, then a year-by-year projection of federal and state income tax to the end of plan.

Withdrawal order
Three tiers, spent from the top down: taxable accounts (non-qualified brokerage, savings and cash), tax-deferred accounts (IRAs, 401(k)s, rollovers and deferred annuities) and Roth accounts. That is the default order — it keeps tax-deferred money compounding and leaves tax-free money for last — and the arrows on each tier move it up or down; Reset to default order puts it back. Each tier lists the accounts from section 06 that fall into it, with balances and a subtotal, so the order is read against the client's actual money. The order drives the tax projection below: whatever Needs and Wants need beyond the plan's income is drawn tier by tier.
Roth conversion analysis
Builds a conversion schedule: each year from the start year until the year before RMDs begin, it converts as much qualified money in the Walls and Roof as fits under the top of the target tax bracket — by default the bracket the household sits in during its first full retirement year — without pushing modified AGI over the first IRMAA threshold once either person is within two years of Medicare (Stay under the IRMAA threshold), since the surcharge is set from income two years back. Convert from picks Roof first or Walls first. The tiles compare the plan with and without the strategy: amount converted, tax on the conversions, lifetime tax saved, Roth and tax-deferred balances at the end of plan, and total assets at the end. A bar chart shows the conversion each year with the tax on it, and the schedule lists every year in the window with what limited it (bracket, IRMAA, funding or nothing left). With Apply the conversion strategy ticked, the tax projection below and the asset projection in section 17 run with the conversions in; untick it to see the plan without them.
Required minimum distributions, with and without the conversion strategy
Under the conversion chart, the client's projected RMDs year by year as two bars per year: the required distribution without converting (blue) beside the one with the strategy (purple), from the year RMDs begin to the end of plan, with the figure without conversions written above every fifth year and the peak. Four tiles give the totals over the plan with and without, the required distributions avoided and their share, and the first RMD year with the amount each way; the note beneath reads the chart in words. The comparison is always drawn — with Apply unticked the purple bars show what the strategy would do and are labelled “not applied”. Hover a year for both figures, the difference and the tax-deferred balance each way. Distributions not needed for spending are reinvested in the Roof in both runs; what changes is how much is forced into taxable income.
Qualified charitable distributions
Gifts to charity paid straight from an IRA from age 70½. Enter each person's annual gift in today's dollars (it grows with inflation and is capped at the IRS limit — $111,000 a person in 2026, indexed — and at the balance), the from age (71 by default, the first full year after 70½) and whether the gifts are already in the spending budget (the QCD replaces cash gifts that sit in Needs and Wants, and the freed cash meets spending or is reinvested like spare RMD money) or new giving on top of it. Each year's QCD comes out of that person's tax-deferred balances, counts toward their required minimum distribution and is excluded from income, so it never raises taxable Social Security, the bracket or IRMAA. Four tiles: the total given from the IRA, the RMD income avoided (the part of each year's required distribution the gift satisfies), the lifetime tax saved against making the same gifts in cash under the standard deduction (where a cash gift is not deductible, so the saving is the full marginal rate on each gift, federal and state), and assets at the end of plan each way. The chart stacks the gift on the taxable RMD left after it, year by year, with the RMD giving in cash instead as a dashed line; hover for the figures and the tax each way that year. With Apply ticked the whole plan — the tax, RMD and asset projections and the report — runs with the QCDs; unticked, the comparison still shows what they would do. The status quo never has them. Each scenario keeps its own settings, and they save with the case. With the Roth strategy on, the conversions re-size around the QCDs, so the end-of-plan asset figure moves with both.
Projected income tax
Five tiles — lifetime federal tax, lifetime state tax, average effective rate (all tax over all income), the highest federal bracket reached and when RMDs begin — then three charts with a bar for every year: taxable income; the effective federal rate, each bar coloured by the highest federal bracket that year reaches (lighter to darker as the rate rises) with the rate written inside; and federal and state tax stacked. On all three the year RMDs begin is picked out with a tinted column and a dashed marker. Beneath them a table for every year to the end of plan: all income, taxable Social Security, withdrawals by tier, RMDs, AGI, deductions, taxable income, the bracket, the federal tax and effective rate, the state tax, all tax and the combined effective rate. Income is the same projection the section 13 chart uses — wages to each retirement date, Social Security, pensions, annuities, non-guaranteed income, the Foundation annuity and the Walls yield — plus required minimum distributions on the tax-deferred market balances and whatever has to be drawn, in the withdrawal order, to meet Needs and Wants and the tax itself. It files jointly with a spouse on file, single otherwise, and takes the standard deduction with the extra amount at 65 (and the $6,000 senior deduction while it lasts, 2025–2028). Hover the chart for any year's figures.

What the tax figures assume

2026 federal law (the One Big Beautiful Bill Act figures), with brackets, the standard deduction and the capital-gains thresholds indexed at the plan's inflation rate; the Social Security thresholds are not indexed, so up to 85% of benefits becomes taxable over time. Wages, pensions, non-guaranteed income, tax-deferred withdrawals and RMDs are ordinary income; a tax-deferred withdrawal taken before 59½ carries the 10% additional federal tax (the birthday from the section 02 date of birth, or taken as mid-year without one; the withdrawal is grossed up for it, the table shows it in its own column and the assumptions line totals it); the Foundation annuity is taxed by its funding (qualified money fully, a non-qualified annuity half, Roth money not at all), and annuity income already in force the same way by the account that pays it — a section 04 line with no account, or a qualified contract, fully; a non-qualified contract half; a Roth contract not at all; a MYGA's interest fully unless the contract is Roth. The non-qualified share of the Walls yield and half of each taxable-account withdrawal are long-term gains, unless the Walls yield is marked federally tax-free in section 14. RMDs follow the Uniform Lifetime Table from 73 or 75 by birth year; an RMD larger than the plan's own tax-deferred draw is taxed, spent first and otherwise reinvested in the after-tax Roof account. Both spouses are assumed living to the end of plan; no itemising, credits or IRMAA.

State tax follows the state in section 02 through a planning model of each state's 2025 rates and brackets, standard deduction or personal exemptions, and retirement-income rules — Pennsylvania, Illinois and Mississippi leave pension, IRA and annuity income untaxed; New York, Georgia, South Carolina, Maryland and others exclude a set amount per person from a given age; Social Security is exempt except where the state taxes it. Nine states have no income tax. Local income taxes (Maryland counties, Ohio and Pennsylvania municipalities, New York City) are not included. The whole projection is a planning estimate, not tax advice, and prints in the worksheet under Projected income tax.

Where an account lands

Tax type decides it: Non-Qualified is taxable, Qualified is tax-deferred, Roth is Roth. Two exceptions: a non-qualified account in the Annuity position is tax-deferred, and a life or LTC policy is left out — it is not a withdrawal source. The order saves with the case and prints in the worksheet under Tax — withdrawal order; it changes the tax projection, not the Fortress sizing.

Projected Retirement Assets

What is left each year after the withdrawals in section 16, in total and by Fortress level, to the end of plan. Five tiles — assets today, at full retirement, at the end of plan, total withdrawn beyond the plan's income, and the peak (or the year the accounts run out) — then a stacked bar for every year: liquidity buffer, Foundation annuity, Walls, Roof, Moat and anything held outside the plan, with the total labelled every fifth year and the year RMDs begin marked. A table beneath carries every year.

How the balances move

Balances start from the plan's own transfers in section 14 and roll forward a year at a time on the section 03 rates: the Walls at the Walls growth rate (after the Walls yield is paid), the Roof at the Roof growth rate, accounts left in place at the Walls rate, the liquidity buffer as cash with no growth. The Foundation annuity falls by the income it pays (and keeps paying for life if Guaranteed lifetime income is Yes); the Moat is an LTC contract, shown at the amount placed while it pays out. Withdrawals are the section 16 draws — what Needs and Wants (and the tax on the draw) need beyond the plan's income — taken in the withdrawal order, cash first within each tier, then Walls, then Roof; RMDs above the plan's own tax-deferred draw are spent first and otherwise reinvested after tax in the Roof. Future dollars with no market variation: a planning estimate, and it prints in the worksheet under Projected retirement assets.

Your Personal Cash Flow Statement

The same projection read as a cash flow, one row a year, beneath the asset chart. Four tiles — income over the plan, expenses and taxes, withdrawals, and net worth at the end of plan — then the table: income by source without touching the accounts (salaries to each retirement date, Social Security, pensions, annuity income, the Walls yield, other income, and RMDs above the plan's own tax-deferred draw; a source with nothing in it is left out), total income, Needs and Wants in future dollars, any care cost paid from the accounts, federal and state tax (Roth conversion tax included), the cash flow before withdrawals, the section 16 withdrawals (grossed up for the tax they cause), the net cash flow, the ending account balance and the ending net worth. The net cash flow is zero in a year the accounts fund and positive in a surplus year; spare RMD money is reinvested in the Roof and other surplus income is not banked, so a surplus does not add to the balances. Net worth is the section 17 total plus the section 06 other assets at their growth rates, less the section 07 debts as their payments retire them (a debt with no payment or interest only stands; one with a maturity date is gone by then). The footer totals every column over the plan. It is for the advisor's analysis and the screen; it does not print in the worksheet or the executive summary.

Sensitivity Analysis

The section 17 projection for the scenario being viewed, run again under three stresses one at a time with everything else unchanged, each with its own tiles, chart and note and the base case drawn as a dashed line:

Sequence of returns risk
The Walls earn −8% and the Roof −16% a year in the first three years of full retirement (from the year after the last retirement date), then return to the assumed rates. The chart marks where the bad years begin; the tiles show the returns, what is withdrawn, the lowest point and where the plan ends against the base case.
Social Security cut
Benefits 22% lower from 2032 — the year the trust fund is projected to be depleted — for everyone, with the tax on Social Security following the reduced benefit. The tiles total the benefit lost over the plan.
Higher inflation
Long-term inflation 30% above the section 03 assumption (4.24% instead of 3.26%, say), applied to Needs and Wants and to the indexing of the tax brackets alike. The tiles total the extra spending over the plan.

The stresses use the scenario's own transfers, annuity income, Roof growth and Roth strategy, so each scenario has its own set; the scenario cards at the top of section 14 show each scenario's end-of-plan assets under all three, side by side. If a long term care event occurs

Under the three stress tests, the same three care-event projections as section 06 — male, female, both — run through the plan as adjusted. The insured's Moat benefit pays first and falls as it does (the Moat bar shrinks); anything above the benefit comes out of the accounts in the withdrawal order, with the tax on the draw. The tiles show the cost of care, how much the Moat paid, what came from the accounts and where the plan ends against the projection without a care event. As in section 06, each person who needs care is assumed to pass away at the end of care, with the survivor's Social Security, pension and annuity income adjusted from then and the plan ending with the second death. Set against the same charts in section 06, they show what the Moat is for.

Transfer sequence and ending positions

Section 19 is the paperwork in order. Step 1 consolidates everything at the new custodian — qualified plans into one Rollover IRA per owner, Roth into one Roth IRA per owner, after-tax into a single joint account. Step 2 moves the Foundation and Moat allocations on to the carriers. A Stays put block covers what never moves. Both steps follow section 14: each source account shows what each part of it is going to, Step 2 names the carrier and product you picked (marked set in 06), and a line sent to a different custodian than the consolidation is flagged as needing its own registration.

Read the flags on each row before you generate paperwork. They catch the things that reject transfers or surprise clients: a registration mismatch, an in-force annuity that will not move by ACATS, a surrender charge worth checking, capital gains from liquidating a taxable position.

Section 20 opens with the asset map: every current account drawn as a box on the outside — the client’s on the left, the spouse’s on the right, joint and trust accounts on whichever side has room — with its name, type and balance, and the ending registrations and contracts in the middle, coloured by Fortress level. A line runs from each account to wherever its money ends up, its width by the amount; an account that splits shows how much goes each way. The map reads the same ledger as the transfer sequence and the ending positions, so the three always agree, and the executive summary carries it on as many pages as the accounts need.

Below the map is where every account lands, plus assets under management and the advisory fee. The fee schedule is flat, not marginal — wherever the managed total falls, that band's rate applies to the whole balance. Billable assets are the Walls and Roof only; the cash buffer, the Foundation annuity and the Moat contracts are out. The weighted average spreads that same cost across everything in the plan, which is the number a client will ask about.

Two rules the tool will not let you break

Long-term care contracts are always individually owned — each insured gets their own policy, even when one joint account funds both, and the tool flags the change of title.

With no spouse on file, nothing is titled Joint or Spouse. If an account is still marked that way you will see a flag naming it; fix it in section 06 or add the spouse.

Saving and reopening a case

Press Copy case data and paste the block into your CRM note, an email to yourself, wherever the case file lives. It carries the household, every account, and any transfers you adjusted by hand.

To pick it back up, paste the block into the same box and press Load pasted case. Everything comes back, including your adjustments, re-pointed at the right accounts.

NotesWorth knowing

Things that catch people out

  • No Cash row, no buffer. The liquidity buffer only funds from accounts marked Cash. If the household's savings came in as Market, the buffer sits empty and the Foundation looks wrong.
  • The LTC cost defaults fight you until you type. Changing a person's age or sex rewrites their average LTC cost — but only until you have edited that field yourself, after which your figure sticks.
  • Yields are assumptions you are publishing. Whatever you set appears in the client summary's disclosures as an assumed rate. Set them to something you can defend.
  • Held-back accounts leave the fee base twice. An account marked Stay as is is out of billable AUM and out of the weighted-average denominator, which raises that percentage. That is deliberate — you should not average a fee across money you are not managing.
  • An account named "Joint brokerage" keeps that name. The tool never titles a registration Joint for a single client, but it will not rename an account you typed. Rename it in section 06 if it should not appear that way on a client document.
  • Refresh is fatal. Worth saying twice.
